ROADMAP / 03 · AI & AUTOMATION

Speeding up the loop with AI, PINNs and automation

Each turn of the loop (make, measure, model, decide) currently takes weeks. The next phase of my program uses physics-informed machine learning and high-throughput automation to run it faster and more often, with the physics built in rather than replaced. planned

ROADMAP / 05 · IMPACT

What this program delivers

For performance
Mechanical design rules

Critical current densities, stack pressures and interface toughness values that set how far Li metal, Si and solid electrolytes can be pushed toward 500 Wh/kg and 15-minute charging.

For materials security
Mechanics of abundant chemistries

Na-ion electrodes built on sodium, which the U.S. produces in large amounts, and cells with less graphite, cobalt and germanium, whose supplies are fully or largely imported or under export restrictions.

For safety
Predicting and reversing shorts

Fracture-based limits on dendrite growth, and ways to heal a dendrite short before it becomes a failure.

For speed
AI-accelerated discovery

Physics-informed models and automated, active-learning experiments that shorten the time from a candidate material to a validated mechanical limit. planned

For the community
Open methods and training

Tutorials, code and data that other labs can reuse, and a record of mentoring undergraduate and pre-college researchers.

ResultBuilt a blister-interferometry test (~130 nm resolution). Interface toughness of PVdF/Si falls from 0.55 to 0.31 J/m² with electrolyte and to 0.26 J/m² after cycling.
EvidenceACS AEM 2025 ↗Exp. Mech. 2023 · cover ↗JAM 2024 ↗

ResultBulk Li₆PS₅Cl: E = 4.7 GPa, KIc ≈ 0.17 MPa·m½, which sets a critical current density estimate for dendrite growth.

    Tutorials / Tutorial 01 · Experimental mechanics

    Seeing nanometres with light

    How a Michelson interferometer tracks a pressurized PVdF film as it bulges and peels, using the setup behind three interface fracture papers.

    TUTORIAL 01 · EXPERIMENTAL MECHANICS

    Measuring blister deflection with a Michelson interferometer

    How the in-house setup behind three interface fracture papers measures how far a pressurized PVdF film bulges, with a resolution of about 130 nm, while the pressure rises until the film peels off.

    • Exp. Mech. 2023
    • J. Appl. Mech. 2024
    • ACS AEM 2025

    1The problem

    A blister test finds the interface toughness Gc from two measurements taken at the moment a crack starts: the pressure p under a free-standing film, and the shape w(r) of the film as it bulges. The film is a few micrometres thick and spans a hole about 1 mm wide, and it deflects only a few micrometres before it delaminates. Measuring that shape without touching the film calls for an optical method: interferometry.

    2The setup

    Inside the illumination arm
    ①
    White light source

    A broadband lamp that sends light out in all directions. On its own it is too spread out and has too many wavelengths to make clear fringes.

    ②
    Collector lens

    Gathers as much of the diverging light as possible and brings it to a focus, so the lamp's output is used instead of wasted.

    ③
    Bandpass filter

    Passes only a narrow green band: 550 ± 10 nm in 2023, 514 ± 1 nm in 2025. One wavelength gives one fringe spacing (λ/2), and a narrower band gives a longer coherence length, so fringes stay visible over larger deflections.

    ④
    Condenser lens

    Placed one focal length beyond the focus, it turns the diverging green light into a collimated beam. Parallel rays reach every point of the sample at the same angle and with even brightness, so a change in brightness means a change in height, not in lighting.

    3Why bright fringes appear

    Light reflected from the film travels an extra distance 2w compared with light from the reference mirror: w on the way down and w on the way back. The two beams add in phase whenever that extra path is a whole number of wavelengths:

    I(x,y) = I₀ [ 1 + γ cos( 4π w(x,y) / λ ) ]intensity at each pixel, where γ is the fringe contrast
    2 w = m λ  ⇒  Δwfringe = λ / 2one fringe = a half-wavelength of deflection
    δwmin ≈ λ / 4resolution, from one peak to the next valley

    5From images to w(r, t)

    1. Record. The camera saves a frame every 200–400 ms, and LabVIEW stamps each frame with the pressure measured at that moment.
    2. Track pixels, not fringes. For each pixel, extract its brightness in every frame. Normalize by the 8-bit maximum.
    3. Count peaks. Each brightness peak adds λ/2 to that pixel's height. In the 2023 paper, 10 peaks in 200 s at the centre pixel meant w₀ ≈ 10 × 275 nm = 2.75 µm.
    4. Build the profile. Repeat for pixels along a radius to get w(r) at each pressure, then compare the p–w₀ curve with the finite element model.
    5. Mark the crack. The same frames show the crack front moving outward. The pressure when about 50 µm of film has peeled off is the crack initiation pressure pc, which is the input for calculating Gc.

    6Knowing when the crack starts

    The fracture calculation needs the crack initiation pressure pc. By eye, it is the pressure at which about 50 µm of delamination is visible in the recorded video. In lithiated specimens, electrolyte left at the interface can hide the crack front, so the 2025 paper added a second check that uses only the p–w₀ curve:

    dw₀/dp rises by 15%  ⇒  p = pconce the crack grows, the free span widens and compliance starts to rise

    In the paper, the two methods gave the same initiation pressure.

    Tutorials / Tutorial 02 · Additive manufacturing

    Where soft meets hard

    Measuring how well 3D-printed TPU sticks to ABS: printing the specimen, detecting crack initiation, and calculating Jc.

    TUTORIAL 02 · ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING

    How strongly does a printed soft layer stick to a hard one?

    A step-by-step guide to measuring interface toughness between 3D-printed ABS (rigid) and TPU (flexible), from printing the specimen to the J-integral, based on Progress in Additive Manufacturing (2024).

    • Prog. Addit. Manuf. 2024
    • Double cantilever beam
    • J-integral

    1The problem

    Multi-material printers can build a rigid frame and a soft skin in one part, for robot joints, prosthetics or haptic surfaces. These parts usually fail where the two materials meet. Measuring how strong that interface is turns out to be hard:

    2300 MPaABS modulus (rigid)
    20 MPaTPU modulus (flexible)
    ≈ 115×stiffness mismatch

    Standard tests such as ASTM D5528 assume the specimen is linear elastic and ignore the soft interlayer. TPU stretches to 560% and blunts the crack tip, so both assumptions fail. This method makes three changes: it detects crack initiation from a stiffness drop instead of by eye, models TPU as hyperelastic, and computes the toughness with the J-integral.

    3Finding the moment the crack starts

    The specimen is loaded at 1 mm/min on a universal testing machine with a 1 kN load cell, and a CMOS camera watches the crack tip. In a rigid ABS/ABS specimen the crack tip stays sharp and you can see it move. In ABS/TPU the TPU blunts and stretches, so the moment the crack starts is hard to see. Instead, the method watches the stiffness of the specimen:

    S = dF / dzstiffness fitted between 10% and 60% of peak force; initiation is taken at a 15% drop

    The 15% threshold was checked against visual crack detection in monolithic ABS/ABS specimens, where the two methods gave statistically the same initiation loads.

    7Takeaways

    1. For rigid/soft interfaces, detect initiation from stiffness, not from images of a blunted crack tip.
    2. Use J-integral with a hyperelastic model for the soft layer. Linear-elastic standards ignore the interlayer and are unreliable here.
    3. Check that Jc does not change with precrack length. That confirms you are measuring the interface and not the specimen geometry.
    4. Report the interlayer thickness with every Jc, because it changes the toughness you measure.

    Stress you can see in a laser spot

    How a multi-beam optical sensor turns a slight bend in a wafer into the stress inside a sodium-ion electrode.

    TUTORIAL 03 · OPERANDO STRESS

    Measuring film stress by watching a wafer bend

    How a multi-beam optical stress sensor (MOSS) measures the stress in a battery electrode while it charges, by tracking how four laser spots move as the substrate bends by a radius of tens of metres. Based on the Ge thin-film and hard-carbon composite papers.

    • J. Electrochem. Soc. 2021
    • J. Power Sources 2024
    • Stoney equation

    1The idea

    When sodium enters an electrode film, the film tries to expand, by more than 300% in amorphous Ge. The film is bonded to a thick substrate that stops it expanding sideways, so the film ends up in compression and pushes on the substrate, which bends slightly. Measure that bending and you know the force per unit width in the film, its stress × thickness, at every moment of the charge cycle.

    10s of mtypical radius of curvature
    2 × 2array of parallel laser beams
    operandomeasured while the cell cycles

    The bending is far too small to see by eye. MOSS reflects a set of parallel laser beams off the substrate. A flat surface keeps them parallel, while a curved one tilts them towards or away from each other. A camera some distance away sees this as a change in the spacing between the spots.

    2The setup

    ①
    Laser

    A solid-state source at 660 nm. A single collimated beam is enough; the etalons do the splitting.

    ②
    Etalons

    Light bouncing between the two faces of each plate leaves as a row of equally spaced, parallel beams. Two plates set at 90° give a 2 × 2 array, so curvature can be measured in two directions and you can check that the film expands equally both ways.

    ③
    Why an array helps

    Vibration or a tilted stage moves all spots together, but only bending changes the spacing between them. Measuring the spacing cancels most noise.

    ④
    Camera and calibration

    The CCD finds the centre of each spot. The "mirror constant" converts spacing change into curvature and is calibrated with a mirror of known curvature (≈ 1.5 in the hard-carbon setup).

    3From spots to stress

    κ = (cos φ / 2L) · (D − D₀) / D₀curvature from the spot spacing, where cos φ / 2L is the mirror constant
    σf = σr + Es ts² κ / [ 6 tf (1 − νs) ]Stoney equation, where σr is the residual stress left from deposition
    tf = tf0 (1 + β z)the film thickens as it sodiates (z = state of charge)

    MOSS measures stress × thickness directly. To get the true stress you need the film's thickness at that moment. For a-Ge, which expands by more than 300%, the Ge paper measured this separately: AFM step heights on patterned films stopped at different states of charge.

    Stoney's assumptions: the film is much thinner than the substrate, the stress is uniform and equal in both in-plane directions, deflections are small, and the substrate stays elastic. A thin sputtered film on a 500 µm wafer meets them well. A 90 µm composite electrode glued on with epoxy does not, so the hard-carbon paper uses a modified Stoney equation with a correction f(ti, Mi) that accounts for the epoxy, Al foil and electrode layers.

    5Reading a stress–capacity curve

    1. Soak first. Record the curvature while the electrode sits in electrolyte before cycling. The binder can absorb solvent and create stress with no current flowing.
    2. Elastic loading. Early in sodiation, stress becomes compressive in proportion to capacity. The slope reflects the film's biaxial modulus and how much it expands.
    3. Plastic flow. The stress levels off: the film cannot hold more compression and flows plastically while it keeps expanding. In a-Ge this looks like lithiation, but at a lower flow stress.
    4. Unloading. When desodiation starts, the stress first reverses elastically, then turns tensile and flows again. The loop this forms is mechanical energy lost in each cycle.
    5. Plot against capacity, not time. That lets you compare different currents, and relate changes in slope to storage mechanisms such as the sloping and plateau regions in hard carbon.

    6Composite electrodes: bonding, layers and soaking

    1. Bond: glue the electrode's Al current collector to an oxidized Si(111) wafer (Ø 50.8 mm) with epoxy, with the coating facing out.
    2. Measure: section a sister specimen and measure every layer thickness by SEM. These thicknesses go straight into the stress calculation.
    3. Assemble: in a Teflon beaker, place a 2 mm Na disc (Ø 50 mm, with a Cu wire pressed in), a Ø 55 mm glass-fibre separator, then the electrode facing down. Add electrolyte up to, but not over, the wafer, and seal the lid with a rubber gasket.
    4. Soak and record: track curvature for about 15 h before any current flows. The paper attributes the stress during this period to electrolyte–binder interactions (see its supplementary S1). Start cycling from this baseline.

    Pressing on infected skin

    Flat-punch indentation, reading the unloading curve, and pull-off work, applied to porcine skin as a bacterial biofilm matures.

    TUTORIAL 04 · BIOMECHANICS

    Flat-punch indentation of soft, sticky tissue

    How to measure the apparent stiffness and stickiness of ex vivo porcine skin as a P. aeruginosa biofilm grows on it, with one custom indenter and a careful reading of the unloading curve. Based on McCall, Pakhare et al., J. Mech. Behav. Biomed. Mater. (2026).

    • JMBBM 2026
    • Flat-punch contact
    • Pull-off work
    • Microneedle puncture

    1The question

    More than 60% of chronic wound infections involve biofilms: bacteria living inside a sticky matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). Microneedles are being developed to push drugs through that matrix, but nobody had measured how the infected skin itself changes mechanically as the biofilm matures. This study grows a biofilm on porcine skin and tests it at 24, 48 and 72 hours, each time next to an uninfected control cut from the neighbouring piece of skin.

    3.25 mmflat stainless-steel punch
    10%of sample thickness indented
    116 µm/sindentation rate

    2The setup

    3From the unloading curve to a modulus

    Skin is viscoelastic, so loading and unloading follow different paths (hysteresis). The start of unloading is the most elastic part of the curve, so the contact stiffness Kmat is taken as the slope of the first 10% of the unloading curve. For a flat circular punch, the contact area stays the same as it lifts, which makes the analysis simple:

    Er = Kmat / Dtipflat-punch contact, where Dtip = 3.25 mm
    1/Er = (1 − νtip²)/Etip + (1 − νmat²)/Ematremoves the tip's own stiffness; νmat = 0.5
    Wpull = −∫ F dδ  (F < 0)pull-off work: the area of the curve below zero force

    Be honest about what this number means. Skin with a biofilm is viscoelastic, sticky and layered, so it is not the elastic half-space the equations assume. Emat is an apparent reduced contact modulus for this probe, rate, depth and analysis window, and Wpull is a working measure of how hard the probe is to detach, not a thermodynamic work of adhesion. Both are good for comparing samples tested the same way. Taking 10% of the unloading curve gave 0.2–0.7 MPa, within the range reported for porcine skin (≈ 0.2–5 MPa).

    3. Treat the numbers as apparent, comparative values. Compare only neighbouring samples tested with the same protocol.
    4. Keep recording once the force drops below zero. The pull-off region tells you about adhesion, which a modulus alone cannot.

    1Why bulk values matter

    Lithium dendrites grow through a solid electrolyte by cracking it. Lithium fills a small flaw, the pressure builds, and the crack grows. Predicting when that happens needs two numbers: the Young's modulus E and the fracture toughness KIc.

    Nanoindentation is the usual way to measure them, but it probes only the surface, at a scale close to the grain size (≈ 1 µm) of a pellet with about 13% porosity. A dendrite, however, cracks the bulk. This work tests millimetre-scale specimens, large enough that the porous material behaves as a uniform continuum.

    7Handling notes and rig checks

    1. Pressing: cold-press the Li₆PS₅Cl powder at 360 MPa in an Ar glovebox into Ø 12.7 mm pellets, 0.55–0.75 mm thick. Then heat at 60 °C for 2 h, which was shown earlier to improve cycling without creating impurity phases.
    2. Porosity: compare the pellet's apparent density (mass divided by volume) with the theoretical density: φ = 1 − ρapp/ρth. The pellets here were 13 ± 2% porous, which is why bulk and surface measurements disagree.
    3. Air exposure: once out of the glovebox, keep specimens under mineral oil, including during wire-saw cutting and drilling. The group had earlier shown that oil protects even more sensitive sulfide glasses over similar times.
    4. Cutting: use a diamond wire saw. The material is too brittle for perfect rectangles, so measure every outline in ImageJ and model that exact shape.
    5. Validate the rig: bend an aluminium alloy beam of known modulus with the same setup. The measured value was within 2%.
    6. Reading the fracture trace: the soft load cell stores elastic energy, so once the crack starts it runs through the specimen, and the load cell rings down in a damped oscillation. Only the peak force is meaningful, because this setup cannot measure a stable crack-growth curve.
    7. Rate window: above about 40 µm/s some discs broke at the pin holes instead of the notch, so discard those tests.

    8Takeaways

    1. For a porous electrolyte, measure bulk properties with specimens much larger than the grains and pores. Surface indentation can overestimate both E and KIc by several times.

    4. Low toughness leads directly to a low critical current density. Mechanics is part of the dendrite problem, not separate from it.

    3Why two etches? Making the precrack

    DRIE (the Bosch process) etches almost straight down. It is fast and makes vertical walls, but its plasma would damage the polymer if it reached it. Cu sputtered up through the hole can't reach into the undercut, so it shows up as a dark ring around the bright Cu disc, and FIB cross-sections confirm there is a gap there.

    1Why a thin film?

    A composite electrode mixes particles, binder, carbon and pores, so any stress or swelling you measure is an average over all of them. A dense sputtered film removes that complexity. It has no binder, a known thickness, a flat surface, and it is bonded to a substrate that stays elastic and does not react. Whatever changes is the active material itself.

    ①
    Fused silica

    500 µm thick, Ø 50.8 mm, polished on both sides. It is electrochemically inert and elastic, so it serves as the curvature sensor.

    ②
    Ti · 5 nm

    An adhesion layer, so the stack stays attached while the film swells to several times its thickness.

    ③
    Cu · 200 nm

    The current collector. Cu does not alloy with Na, so it adds no stress of its own.

    ④
    a-Ge · 30–120 nm

    The active material. Sputtered Ge is amorphous, with no grains or texture to complicate the result.

    3Confirm what you made

    4A patterned film for measuring swelling

    Measuring how much a film thickens needs a reference surface right next to it, one that never changes height. The answer is to pattern the stack so that bare silica is exposed beside it. This uses lift-off: the resist pattern is made first, the metal is deposited over everything, and the resist is then dissolved, taking the unwanted metal with it.

    Patterned stack: Ti 5 nm / Ni 75 nm / Ge 30 nm (≈ 110 nm total) on a 25 × 25 mm polished fused-silica piece. Ti and Ni don't react with Na, so any change in step height comes from the Ge. Deposited by e-beam evaporation, with Microposit remover for lift-off. Edge spikes seen in AFM are likely lift-off tearing, so measure away from the edges.

    5Measuring the step height by AFM, without air

    1. Stop cells at a known state: after the first sodiation, after one full cycle, and after the second sodiation, with at least three samples each.
    2. Clean: open the cell in the glovebox, rinse in propylene carbonate for 10 min, and dry for 24 h in the same glovebox.
    3. Transfer: move the samples to a second glovebox that houses the AFM (Bruker Dimension Icon), so they never touch air.
    4. Scan: use PeakForce Tapping with a ScanAsyst-Air probe (k ≈ 0.4 N/m), measuring from the bare silica across onto the film at two or more locations per sample.
    5. Average: sodiated films are rough, so average the height over the scan length rather than taking a single point.

    Why this matters for my work. The anode material I study for Na-ion batteries, germanium, is now under export restrictions, and graphite, the standard Li-ion anode, is fully imported. That strategic case for Na-ion, together with hard carbon and Prussian Blue electrodes made from abundant elements, is part of why understanding the mechanics of Na-ion electrodes matters.

    MATERIALS / 04

    Where battery materials are heading

    Each move to a higher-energy material makes the mechanics harder: bigger volume changes, stiffer and more brittle electrolytes, and more reactive interfaces. These are the open problems my methods are built for.

    This is an outlook written from the general literature, not a claim about my own results. The mechanics questions are the directions I plan to pursue.

    Research / Vision

    Mechanics-informed energy storage

    Batteries rarely fail from chemistry alone. Interfaces delaminate, electrolytes crack and electrodes swell. My program measures and models these failures so that next-generation batteries can be built from safe, abundant and secure materials.

    VISION / 01 · THE CHALLENGE

    Three pressures on the next battery

    Performance

    DOE targets: up to 500 Wh/kg and charging in under 15 minutes. Both push toward Li metal, silicon and solid electrolytes, the materials most likely to fail mechanically.

    DOE Battery500 · DOE VTO
    Security

    The U.S. imports 100% of its natural graphite, 79% of its cobalt and >50% of its lithium. Germanium exports to the U.S. are banned; 60 minerals are on the 2025 critical list.

    USGS MCS 2026 · Federal Register, Nov 2025
    Safety

    Dendrites short cells, and liquid electrolytes turn a short into a fire. Solid electrolytes remove the liquid but crack: measured toughness of Li₆PS₅Cl is only 0.17 MPa·m½.

    ACS AEM 2025

    All three meet at one question: do the materials survive mechanically while they work?
